# Differentials, residues, and Riemann–Roch

Ninth of the `base/` notes. [008](008-divisors-and-pic0.md) built divisors,
linear equivalence and $`\mathrm{Pic}^0`$, and stated the residue theorem
and the genus as facts. This note supplies the machinery behind those two facts:
the differentials of a curve, their orders and divisors, local residues and the
residue theorem, and the two inequalities — Riemann's and Riemann–Roch — that
turn a degree count into a dimension count.

That conversion is the reason the section matters for FLT. For the modular curve
it is what turns degree counts on $`J_0(N)`$ into dimensions of spaces of
functions and differentials, feeding the Hecke action of
[math/009](../math/009-hecke-jacobian-commute.md) and the rank-$`2g`$ count of
[008 §4](008-divisors-and-pic0.md).

Math first: §§1–4 are the classical theory, §5 is the computation carried out
for genera $`0`$ to $`4`$ in `pymath/riemann_roch.py`, and §6 summarizes the Lean
encoding (everything here is again FLT's own layer; mathlib supplies only the
Kähler differentials $`\Omega_{F/K}`$).

Line-number citations point at `anthropics/fermats-last-theorem@aa2d8b3`; mathlib
citations point at tag **v4.33.0**. Both are rendered GitHub links.

## 1. Differentials of a curve

Let $`C`$ be a smooth projective curve over $`K`$, with function field
$`F = K(C)`$. The **differentials** of $`C`$ are the Kähler differentials

$$\Omega_{F/K} \\;=\\; \Omega^1_{F/K},$$

the $`F`$-vector space generated by symbols $`df`$ ($`f \in F`$) subject to
$`d(fg) = f\,dg + g\,df`$, $`d(f+g) = df + dg`$ and $`da = 0`$ for $`a \in K`$.
For a curve this space is **one-dimensional** over $`F`$: the derivative of a
single separating element generates it. That single dimension is what makes the
divisor theory of differentials work, exactly as the single maximal ideal of a
point made the divisor theory of functions work in [008 §1](008-divisors-and-pic0.md).

Fix a point $`P`$ and a uniformizer $`\pi_P`$ (008 §1). Since $`\Omega_{F/K}`$
is one-dimensional, every $`\omega \in \Omega_{F/K}`$ can be written locally as

$$\omega \\;=\\; f\\,\mathrm{d}\pi_P$$

for a unique $`f \in F`$, and we define the **order of $`\omega`$ at $`P`$** by

$$\mathrm{ord}_P(\omega) \\;:=\\; \mathrm{ord}_P(f).$$

This does not depend on the choice of uniformizer: another uniformizer is
$`\pi'_P = u\,\pi_P`$ with $`u`$ a unit, and then
$`\mathrm{d}\pi'_P = (u + \pi_P u')\,\mathrm{d}\pi_P`$, where the factor in
parentheses is again a unit — so the order of the coefficient is unchanged.
Because $`f`$ is a function, only finitely many points have
$`\mathrm{ord}_P(\omega) \neq 0`$, and we may sum:

$$\mathrm{div}(\omega) \\;=\\; \sum_P \mathrm{ord}_P(\omega)\\,[P],$$

the **divisor of the differential** $`\omega`$. Multiplying $`\omega`$ by a
nonzero function multiplies the divisor by the divisor of that function,

$$\mathrm{div}(g\\,\omega) \\;=\\; \mathrm{div}(g) + \mathrm{div}(\omega),$$

so the *divisor* changes but the *linear equivalence class* does not. That class
is the **canonical class** $`K_C \in \mathrm{Pic}(C)`$, and any divisor in
it is a **canonical divisor**. Its degree is determined by the curve alone,

$$\deg K_C \\;=\\; 2g - 2,$$

which is the formula that defines the **genus**

$$g \\;=\\; \frac{\deg K_C + 2}{2}.$$

Equivalently, the canonical class is the class of $`\mathrm{div}(\omega)`$
for any nonzero $`\omega`$, and the genus is read off from any representative;
this is exactly how [008 §7](008-divisors-and-pic0.md) states it, and it is how
the code defines $`g`$ (§6). In genus $`1`$ the canonical class has degree zero;
in genus $`0`$ it is the class of $`-2[P]`$ for any point, and the divisor of
$`\mathrm{d}x`$ on the projective line is the model case.

## 2. Residues and the residue theorem

Differentials are not only counted, they are integrated locally. Write
$`\omega = f\,\mathrm{d}\pi_P`$ and expand $`f`$ in powers of the uniformizer,

$$f \\;=\\; \sum_{n \ge -m} a_n\\,\pi_P^{\\,n}, \qquad
  a_n \in K \text{ (the residue field, in general)},$$

with only finitely many negative terms. The **residue** of $`\omega`$ at $`P`$ is
the coefficient of the simple pole,

$$\mathrm{res}_P(\omega) \\;:=\\; a_{-1}.$$

Three properties are what make residues useful, and the first two are immediate
from the definition:

- additivity: $`\mathrm{res}_P(\omega+\omega')=\mathrm{res}_P(\omega)+\mathrm{res}_P(\omega')`$;
- vanishing on holomorphic differentials:
  $`\mathrm{ord}_P(\omega) \ge 0 \Rightarrow \mathrm{res}_P(\omega) = 0`$;
- invariance under a change of uniformizer: the coefficient of
  $`\pi_P^{-1}`$ in $`\omega/\mathrm{d}\pi_P`$ does not depend on which local
  parameter is used. This one is a classical computation — the transformation
  rule for $`\mathrm{d}\pi_P`$ together with the expansion of the unit — and it
  is what makes the residue a property of the *differential*, not of the
  coordinate.

The global statement is the **residue theorem**:

$$\sum_{P \in C} \mathrm{res}_P(\omega) \\;=\\; 0
  \qquad\text{for every nonzero } \omega \in \Omega_{F/K}.$$

The sum is finite because a differential has finitely many poles. The case that
interests us is the logarithmic derivative: for a nonzero function $`f`$,

$$\mathrm{res}_P\\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}f}{f}\right)
  \\;=\\; \mathrm{ord}_P(f),$$

since it differs from $`\mathrm{ord}_P(f)\,\mathrm{d}\pi_P/\pi_P`$ by a holomorphic
form. Applying the residue theorem to $`\mathrm{d}f/f`$ gives
the statement [008 §3](008-divisors-and-pic0.md) used as its foundation:

$$f \neq 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \deg \mathrm{div}(f)
  \\;=\\; \sum_P \mathrm{ord}_P(f) \\;=\\; 0 .$$

So the residue theorem *is* "a nonzero function has as many zeros as poles",
once one knows that residues of logarithmic derivatives are orders. This is why
the code can take the degree-zero property of principal divisors as part of what
it means for $`F/K`$ to be a curve (§6).

Over $`\mathbb{C}`$, residues are also the periods of the differential: the
integral of $`\omega`$ around a loop is the sum of residues in the enclosed
region, and the residue theorem says that summing over all points leaves
nothing. That is the Abel–Jacobi picture behind
[008 §4](008-divisors-and-pic0.md), and it is not needed for the algebra.

## 3. The space $`L(D)`$ and the two inequalities

Let $`D`$ be a divisor on $`C`$ (008 §2). The functions whose poles are bounded
by $`D`$ form a $`K`$-vector space

$$L(D) \\;=\\; \\{\\, f \in F^\times : \mathrm{div}(f) + D \ge 0 \\,\\}
  \cup \\{0\\}, \qquad \ell(D) \\;=\\; \dim_K L(D) \in \mathbb{N}.$$

Three elementary facts set the stage. $`L(D)`$ depends only on the linear
equivalence class of $`D`$ (multiplying $`f`$ by the function realizing
$`D' - D`$ is an isomorphism); $`D \le D'`$ implies
$`L(D) \subseteq L(D')`$, hence $`\ell(D) \le \ell(D')`$; and $`\ell(D) = 0`$
whenever $`\deg D \lt 0`$, since a nonzero function in $`L(D)`$ would have
$`\deg \mathrm{div}(f) + \deg D \ge 0`$ with a negative left-hand term.
The constant functions show $`\ell(0) = 1`$.

The complement to $`L(D)`$ in the count is the space attached to the canonical
divisor:

$$i(D) \\;:=\\; \ell(K_C - D),$$

the **index of specialty** of $`D`$. The two dimensions are related by
**Riemann–Roch**:

$$\ell(D) - \ell(K_C - D) \\;=\\; \deg D + 1 - g .$$

Two consequences are used constantly. First, because
$`\ell(K_C - D) \ge 0`$, Riemann–Roch gives the **Riemann inequality**

$$\ell(D) \\;\ge\\; \deg D + 1 - g,$$

with **equality as soon as $`\deg D \gt 2g - 2`$**: then
$`\deg(K_C - D) \lt 0`$ and the index of specialty vanishes. Second, evaluating
at the two distinguished divisors recovers the two facts of §1:

- $`D = 0`$: $`\ell(0) - \ell(K_C) = 1 - g`$, and $`\ell(0) = 1`$ gives
  $`\ell(K_C) = g`$ — the canonical divisor has exactly $`g`$ independent
  differentials;
- $`D = K_C`$: $`g - 1 = \deg K_C + 1 - g`$, i.e. $`\deg K_C = 2g - 2`$.

So the genus is not an extra input: it is defined by the canonical degree and
then *measured* by the dimension of the canonical space, or by the rank of
$`\mathrm{Pic}^0`$ ([008 §4](008-divisors-and-pic0.md)).

The two halves of Riemann–Roch have different characters, and the code keeps
them apart (§6). The inequality is the half that follows from the adelic/index
description of $`L(D)`$ without any duality; the identity needs **Serre
duality**: the statement that $`i(D)`$ is the dimension of the space of
differentials attached to $`K_C - D`$ — and it is duality that upgrades the
inequality to Riemann–Roch.

## 4. The cases that matter: genus 0, genus 1, and degree zero

Three specializations of §3 are worth writing out, because they are the ones the
proof and the computation meet.

**Degree zero.** If $`\deg D = 0`$ then $`\ell(D) \le 1`$, and $`\ell(D) = 1`$
exactly when $`D`$ is principal. Indeed
$`\ell(D) = \deg D + 1 - g + \ell(K_C - D) = 1 - g + \ell(K_C - D)`$, and for
$`D`$ principal $`K_C - D \sim K_C`$ has $`\ell = g`$, so $`\ell(D) = 1`$;
conversely $`\ell(D) \ge 1`$ together with $`\deg D = 0`$ gives an effective
divisor $`\mathrm{div}(f) + D`$ of degree zero, which must be $`0`$ and
hence makes $`D`$ principal. This is the classification
[008 §3](008-divisors-and-pic0.md) used to identify
$`\mathrm{Pic}^0`$ with the curve in genus one; Riemann–Roch is where it
comes from.

**Genus 0.** On the projective line, $`K_C = -2[P]`$ for any point $`P`$, and
for $`D = n[P]`$ the index of specialty vanishes, so
$`\ell(n[P]) = n + 1`$: the functions are the polynomials of degree at most
$`n`$, with basis $`1, x, \dots, x^n`$. In particular every point is a
$`K`$-point's worth of the same statement, and there are no gaps — every pole
order occurs.

**Genus 1.** Here $`\deg K_C = 0`$ and $`\ell(K_C) = 1`$, so the canonical
divisor is principal: $`K_C = 0`$ in $`\mathrm{Pic}(C)`$. For a point
$`P`$ and $`D = n[P]`$ with $`n \ge 1`$, the divisor $`K_C - D = -n[P]`$ has
negative degree, so $`\ell(K_C - D) = 0`$ and

$$\ell(n[P]) \\;=\\; n \qquad (n \ge 1), \qquad \ell(0) = 1 .$$

The jump from $`\ell(0) = 1`$ to $`\ell([P]) = 1`$ says that there is no
function with a simple pole at $`P`$ and no other poles: the pole order $`1`$ is
a **gap** at $`P`$, and at a rational point of an elliptic curve it is the only
gap. There are exactly $`g`$ gaps at every point of a curve of genus $`g`$;
what distinguishes a Weierstrass point is how *large* they are, not how many.
§5 counts gaps explicitly for the hyperelliptic family.

## 5. The computation for genera $`0`$ to $`4`$

The computation is carried out in
[`../pymath/riemann_roch.py`](../pymath/riemann_roch.py); its output
[`../pymath/riemann_roch.expected.txt`](../pymath/riemann_roch.expected.txt) is
written as an explanation, and its $`15`$ `check` lines are the statements of
§3 evaluated. The setting is chosen so that $`\ell(D)`$ can be computed by
hand: a **hyperelliptic** curve

$$y^2 \\;=\\; f(x), \qquad \deg f = 2g + 1,$$

whose unique point at infinity $`P`$ is a rational Weierstrass point. The
monomials $`x^i y^j`$ are regular away from $`P`$, and at $`P`$ the monomial
$`x^i y^j`$ has pole order

$$\text{ord}_P(x^i y^j) \\;=\\; 2i + (2g+1)j,$$

so the monomials of order at most $`n`$ lie in $`L(n[P])`$ — but they are *not*
independent: the Weierstrass relation $`y^2 = f(x)`$ makes $`y^2 x^i`$ and
$`x^{i+2g+1}`$ have the same pole order, so their difference has smaller order.
A **basis** therefore has exactly one function per *distinct* order:

$$\ell(n[P]) \\;=\\; \\#\\{\\, 2i + (2g+1)j \le n \\,\\},$$

counted without multiplicity — the number of **non-gaps** at most $`n`$. The
demo checks both the spanning set and the dimension, and its point is that the
two counts differ by exactly these repeats.

The cases it walks through:

- **genus $`0`$**: $`\ell(n[P]) = n + 1`$ for $`n = 0, \dots, 4`$, with basis
  $`1, x, \dots, x^n`$, and $`K_C = -2[P]`$;
- **genus $`1`$**: the elliptic curve $`y^2 = x^3 - x`$ over $`\mathbb{F}_7`$ —
  the curve of [008 §6](008-divisors-and-pic0.md) — where $`\ell(n[O]) = n`$
  for $`n \ge 1`$, $`\ell(0) = 1`$, $`K_C = 0`$, and the single gap at $`O`$ is
  the pole order $`1`$;
- **genera $`2, 3, 4`$**: a table of both sides of Riemann–Roch on
  $`D = n[P]`$, with $`\deg K_C = 2g-2`$, $`\ell(K_C) = g`$, the Riemann
  inequality, and equality exactly in the range $`\deg D \gt 2g - 2`$.

For genus $`2`$ and $`3`$ it also verifies the spanning-versus-basis discrepancy
of the paragraph above, which is the concrete form of "a basis has one function
per non-gap". And for every genus $`0`$ to $`4`$ it checks the two relations
$`\deg K_C = 2g - 2`$ and $`g = (\deg K_C + 2)/2`$ — the definition of the genus
in §1, evaluated.
